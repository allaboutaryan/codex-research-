# Preliminary evidence brief: first-time open-source contribution

Status: **CTO desk review; not an agent-team result or QA-approved product decision.** Prepared 2026-10-02. The five-role research task is queued in Northstar Lab, but the local Claude worker reported a limit error before the research and independent-review stages completed.

## Question and boundaries

What reusable software might help people make a useful first contribution to an open-source project? This is a targeted scan of relevant work *published* from 2024-10-02 through 2026-10-02, not an exhaustive two-year systematic review. I checked publisher, university, or conference records and GitHub's own product documentation. No papers or figures are copied into this repository; links point to the originals. Further work must search multiple scholarly indexes, log inclusion/exclusion decisions, inspect full methods, and check competing products before a go decision.

## Synthesis and build hypothesis

The strongest cross-source inference is that a newcomer needs more than another issue list: they need a reliable path from choosing an appropriate task, through a working environment and a place to ask, to a maintainer response. This is an **inference**, not a measured effect of a proposed product. In particular, [GitHub already surfaces `good first issue` labels](https://docs.github.com/en/communities/setting-up-your-project-for-healthy-contributions/encouraging-helpful-contributions-to-your-project-with-labels?apiVersion=2022-11-28) and [provides Discussions for Q&A](https://docs.github.com/en/discussions/collaborating-with-your-community-using-discussions/about-discussions). A generic issue finder or chat forum would duplicate existing features.

**Candidate MVP to validate, not yet to build at scale:** a read-only *Contributor Readiness* web app for maintainers and first-time contributors. For one opted-in GitHub repository, it would display a single task's current prerequisites, the exact setup/test commands sourced from the repository, whether those instructions were recently checked by a human, the maintainer's stated review availability, and a direct handoff to the repository's own issue or Discussion. A newcomer can mark the step where they get stuck and send a concise diagnostic to maintainers; the maintainer sees aggregate friction by step, not private terminal logs. The product's distinctive hypothesis is **verified readiness and feedback at the handoff**, not merely issue discovery, remote IDE hosting, or automatic PR generation. GitHub [already tells contributors to inspect setup requirements](https://docs.github.com/en/get-started/exploring-projects-on-github/contributing-to-open-source), so the opportunity must be validated against its built-in flow and existing tools.

### First experiment and stop criteria

1. Interview 8–12 newcomers and 5–8 maintainers from at least three distinct repositories. Ask for the *last actual* onboarding attempt, failed step, time to first test run, time to first helpful response, and whether existing GitHub/Codespaces tooling sufficed. Do not lead with the proposed app.
2. Manually prepare readiness cards for 10 real starter issues across three consenting repositories. Have maintainers verify commands and review availability; do not scrape private repos or automatically publish issue comments.
3. Run a small usability test comparing the existing repo onboarding path with a readiness card. Primary metric: proportion of participants who can reach a passing local or Codespaces test and correctly identify the next human contact within a fixed session. Secondary metrics: time to first passing test, blocked-step frequency, maintainer correction time, and accepted first contribution after follow-up. Record baseline, sample size, and attrition.
4. **Stop or revise** if maintainers cannot keep cards current, if the existing GitHub workflow performs as well, or if the card increases work without improving successful setup. A short usability study cannot establish long-term retention or market demand.
